Marketing in a Digital World

How the internet, smartphones and 3D printers are rewriting each of the 4Ps.

Prof. Aric Rindfleisch4 modulesM1 Product · M2 Promotion · M3 Placement · M4 Price

Summary

The foundation course. It deliberately skips tactics like SEO and social analytics and instead asks a strategic question: what happens to the classic marketing mix (Product, Promotion, Placement, Price) when the tools used to execute it become digital and democratised?

Each module takes one P, explains the textbook version, then studies two digital concepts that are changing it, one case company, and current academic research. The running argument is that power is shifting from firms to customers: customers now design products, create the ads, manufacture goods on their desktops and even choose the price.

Big idea. Digitisation and democratisation are moving customers from passive buyers to active co-producers across all 4Ps, so firms must change their roles too.

Module notes

Module 1

Product

From firm-created to co-created products and brands
Product & positioning
A product is anything that meets a need or want (good, service or idea). It wins through a distinctive selling proposition, i.e. its positioning.
Stage-gate development
The traditional, secretive, firm-run process: discovery → idea screen → scoping → business case → development → testing → launch, with approval gates between stages.
Customer co-creation
Customers contribute ideas and/or select which ideas get made, usually through a web platform. Classified on a 2×2 of who controls contribution vs selection: collaborating, tinkering, co-designing, submitting.
Sharing economy
Platforms connect people who have under-used assets with people who need them (Airbnb, Uber, Rent the Runway, LendingClub). Access replaces ownership, and accessed brands create weaker attachment.

Case / expert

Threadless: a T-shirt company with no design staff; customers submit and vote on designs. A pioneer of co-creation that later lost momentum.

Research insights

  • Allen, Chandrasekaran & Basuroy (2018, JM): design crowdsourcing on platforms like Crowdspring.
  • Liljedal & Dahlen (2018): use customers to contribute novel ideas, but let them select ideas close to current offerings.
  • Perren & Kozinets (2018, JM): typology of 'lateral exchange markets'.

Practical tips

  • Let the community reject ideas so the firm is not 'the bad guy' (Dell IdeaStorm).
  • Leverage prosumers: they supply assets and also communicate and screen.
  • Sell sharing on lower price and value, not on saving the world.
Module 2

Promotion

From selling products to telling stories
Promotion & ELM
Promotion is persuasive communication about a product. The Elaboration Likelihood Model has two routes: central (reasoned, cognitive) and peripheral (cues like celebrities, humour).
User-generated content (UGC)
Content made by customers rather than the firm. It costs little, is trusted more than paid ads, keeps content fresh and is linked to higher sales.
Doppelgänger brand image (DBI)
A set of disparaging images and stories about a brand spread by a loose network of critics (e.g. parodies of Starbucks, the United Airlines 're-accommodated' backlash). Both a threat and an early warning.

Case / expert

GoPro: cameras whose customers film and share extreme-sports footage, turning users into the brand's main promotional engine.

Research insights

  • Huang et al. (2019, Mgmt Science): which feedback (cooperative, individualistic, competitive) motivates people to create UGC.
  • Thompson, Rindfleisch & Arsel (2006, JM): coined the doppelgänger brand image using Starbucks.

Practical tips

  • Ask customers to share and make it a two-sided conversation.
  • Integrate UGC with traditional ads (Target's college-acceptance campaign).
  • Monitor digital cues for DBIs and answer with authentic content (Blendtec's 'Will It Blend?').
Module 3

Placement

From long physical channels to short digital ones
Placement
Making a product conveniently accessible. Strategy choices include intensive vs extensive distribution and self-service vs full-service retail.
New retail & omnichannel
Online and physical retail are blending: Tesco's subway virtual stores in Korea, b8ta's showroom-style stores, Amazon Go. Physical wins on returns and service; online on research and price.
Desktop manufacturing (3D printing)
'Objects become downloads.' Designs travel as files and are printed locally (e.g. a $10 printable mask in 2020), shrinking the channel to almost nothing.

Case / expert

Tesla: sells direct through company-owned galleries and online, skipping the dealer network.

Research insights

  • Sciandra, Inman & Stephen (2019, JAMS): in-store smartphone use often distracts shoppers from their plan.
  • Rindfleisch, Malter & Fisher (2019): self-manufacturing via 3D printing can cut retailer inventory but also bypass retailers; letting consumers print raises liking and willingness to pay, more so for hedonic products.

Practical tips

  • Market to shoppers once they are inside the store (shopper marketing).
  • Use pop-up stores for low-cost, high-attention presence.
  • Make the physical digital; 'done is the engine of more' in rapid prototyping.
Module 4

Price

Customers taking more control of what they pay
Price captures value
Product, promotion and placement create value for the customer; price captures it for the firm. Set from cost, willingness to pay and competitor prices.
Pay What You Want (PWYW)
Customers decide the price (Radiohead's In Rainbows, Wikipedia donations). Works best for a limited time, with low marginal cost, and with a charity link. Not a sustainable long-term model.
Freemium
A free basic tier plus a paid premium tier (Spotify, Dropbox, New York Times). Free users add value by upgrading and through word of mouth; one free user is worth roughly 15–25% of a premium subscriber.

Case / expert

Panera Cares: pay-what-you-want cafés. About 90% paid something, but stores ran roughly 30% losses and the model was dropped.

Research insights

  • Kim et al. (2009, JM): PWYW as a participative pricing mechanism.
  • Viglia et al.: people pay more after experiencing the product than before, because uncertainty falls.
  • Hamari et al. (2020): upgrades are driven by perceived quality, enjoyment, social and economic value.
  • Niemand, Mai & Kraus (2019): the zero-price effect; clearly explain premium benefits to raise conversion.

Practical tips

  • Use PWYW as a change of pace, not a permanent price.
  • Tie PWYW to charity to signal generosity.
  • Freemium cuts up-front marketing cost because 'free' spreads itself.

Key frameworks

The 4PsProduct, Promotion, Placement, Price as the marketer's toolbox.
Co-creation 2×2Firm vs customer control of contribution and selection.
ELMCentral vs peripheral routes to persuasion.
Stage-gateLinear, gated product development.

Key takeaways

  1. The 4Ps still frame marketing, but each one is now executed with digital, democratised tools.
  2. Customers have become co-creators of products (Threadless, LEGO Ideas) and of promotion (UGC, GoPro).
  3. Critics can co-create too: doppelgänger brand images are free market research if you watch for them.
  4. Channels are shortening: omnichannel retail and 3D printing turn objects into downloads.
  5. Price is becoming participative (PWYW, freemium), but sustainability and profitability need guardrails.
  6. Marketing is shifting from selling products to telling stories.
